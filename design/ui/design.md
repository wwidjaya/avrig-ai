# avrig.ai interface

Status: unreleased, living at `design/ui/` root. No version has been assigned, so nothing is numbered.

Direction: **the product, at rest.** A quiet graphite and off-white surface where the software engineering board is the hero, not an illustration of one. Actions are electric blue, because that is what a person presses to start something. Two colours sit outside that: a deep green that appears only where a human approval happens, and a red that appears only where something is destroyed.

Four surfaces are designed and built as one prototype:

| Surface | File | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Landing | `prototype/index.html` | What the product is, and what a person has to sign off |
| Identity provider | `prototype/iam.html` | Where you actually type a credential |
| Workspace | `prototype/workspace.html` | The project at rest: the lifecycle stage by stage, the approval gate, a conversation about it, and its files on demand |
| App gate (kept, not linked) | `prototype/login.html` | The hand-off page an earlier pass designed. The landing now goes straight to the identity provider, so this is history rather than a step |

Nothing in `apps/` was changed. An earlier pass edited `apps/web` directly and was reverted, so the application still runs its previous pages until a plan assigns the port to `fe`.

## What changed and why

The page being replaced is a dark terminal: animated tickers, `STATUS: ONLINE`, `14 Agents`, `16 / 16 Invariant`, `< 15 ms`. None of those figures exist in the product, and PRODUCT.md forbids presenting invented telemetry as measurement.

Three metaphor directions were built first (a quick reference card, a patent filing, a split-flap departure board). All three looked old: borrowed paper and metal scenery instead of a modern software surface. They are dropped.

What replaced them: the real interface, built with the palette and vocabulary the workspace is moving to, with every example labelled as an example.

## Shared chrome

The header, the two control buttons, the version and the button styles live in one file each, so four surfaces cannot drift apart:

- `prototype/chrome.css` holds the token table, the base reset, the sticky header, the two controls, the version mark and the filled, outlined and approval button styles. Every surface loads it and adds only its own layout.
- `prototype/chrome.js` owns the theme choice, the language choice, the version text and the `avrig:copy` event. Each page declares its own strings in `window.PAGE_COPY` under `en` and `id`, then listens for `avrig:copy` to redraw itself in the new language.
- `prototype/auth.css` holds the auth frame, the field, the reveal button, the notice and the identity provider foot. The app gate and the identity provider screen share it, so the two cannot disagree about a 44px field.
- `prototype/theme-init.js` runs before first paint and applies the stored theme, so there is no flash. No stored choice means **system**, which is also the default the toggle shows.

Language and theme survive navigation between the four pages because they are stored under `avrig-theme` and `avrig-lang` and re-read on load. Signing in at the app gate and landing on the workspace keeps both.

## Structure

### Landing

1. Header. Wordmark, three section anchors, the two control buttons, primary action. No version here: the header is identity and navigation, and the build is stated once in the footer.
2. Hero. Claim on the left, a still of the software engineering board on the right. The claim is that agents do the work and a person signs it off; the board shows exactly that, with the Development row in review and the rest downstream. The bar above the still names the project the workspace prototype opens, `My Software Engineering project`, so the landing still and the workspace are the same project rather than two unrelated examples. Under 400px the bar tightens its padding rather than clipping that name.
3. Lifecycle. Five stages as a vertical tab list (Requirements, Architecture, Development, Test and verification, Deployment). Selecting one shows what it produces, who approves it, and what it holds back. Arrow keys, Home and End move between stages. These five are the landing's summary of the path, and the board chip says `Requirements to deployment` rather than a count: the workspace runs nine stages, so a chip reading `5 stages` beside a product that shows nine would be a number that depends on which page you are on. The landing names the span and leaves the count to the screen that has it.
4. Approval. The gate stated as a record: signer, version, timestamp, the deliberation it rests on, and a note that an agent request to approve returns an error.
5. Surfaces. Two cards: a deliberation transcript, and knowledge results where one answer is an approved baseline and one is agent inference with no source.
6. Closing action and footer.

All three launch buttons are real anchors to the identity provider at `/iam`. The app gate page is kept in the folder so the hand-off states still have a record, but nothing routes through it: one extra page between the landing and the field is a click that answers nothing, and its own content (the issuer row, the "only a human approves" rule) says the same thing the provider screen says under its title. Every filled button is the same blue now, the two `Open the workspace` actions and the short `Workspace` button in the header alike: a filled button is the thing a page asks you to press, and one colour for all of them is what the reader learns in one glance. The header button stays smaller, so the hero still leads, but it no longer carries a different colour to say so. The still of the board in the hero now ends at Deployment, five rows, because the lifecycle the workspace shows ends there too.

### Sign in (the app gate, kept as history)

One card, centred, in a single column: title, notice area, `Continue with SSO`, the issuer row, the rule that only a human account can approve, and a note that the button moves to the identity provider screen. States: default, submitting, provider failure, session expired, declined, reachable at `?state=error`, `?state=expired` and `?state=declined`.

The screen is unlinked but not deleted. If a later decision puts a hand-off page back between the landing and the provider, this is the one that was reviewed; until then the landing goes straight to the password field. Its failure state still has exactly one action: the notice carries no second "try again" button, because the primary button below it already is that action.

### Identity provider

The screen people actually type into, served by `iam.avrig.ai` and not by the app. It is a separate surface because the app never sees a password: the page before this one says so, and this one collects one.

1. `Sign in to avrig.ai`, then one line: use the account this deployment gave you.
2. Notice area, absent until a state produces one.
3. Email, then password, both 44px, with `autocomplete="username"` and `autocomplete="current-password"` so a password manager fills them. The host is in the footer rather than in a sentence.
4. `Show` sits inside the password field and lifts the mask. Its word and its `aria-label` both change to `Hide`, and the field keeps focus so typing continues.
5. One row under the field: `Keep me signed in` with a checkbox, and `Forgot your password?` at the other end. They share a line because a person does not need both at once, and two rows there would push the sign-in button down the card. The checkbox is unchecked by default; a remembered session is a choice, not the default.
6. `Sign in`, full width, with a spinner and `aria-busy` while it waits.
7. A rule, then `Use a passkey` as the other credential.
8. `or continue with`, on a rule, then `Continue with Google` and `Continue with Apple`, in that order, full width, each with its own brand mark in its own colours. A recoloured brand mark is a different logo, so neither follows the page's ink. They sit at the foot of the card because the address this deployment gave is the first way in and the form is what most readers came for; someone without one scans down to the badge they recognise.
9. A plain note that the form is a prototype.

Native validation does the empty-field work: the browser blocks the submit, focuses the first empty field, and `:user-invalid` colours the border. A short script keeps `aria-invalid` on the same beat, because the browser does not sync the two. A server answer cannot be blamed on one field, so it arrives as a card-level notice: wrong email or password, account turned off, too many attempts, session ended. The first three are errors and announce themselves; the last is a status.

States: default, empty field, wrong credentials, account turned off, too many attempts, session ended, password revealed, submitting, passkey note, reset note, Google note, Apple note. `?state=invalid`, `?state=locked`, `?state=ratelimit` and `?state=expired` reach four of them for review.

Three things here are placeholders rather than capabilities: the two provider buttons, the passkey button and the reset link. Pressing Google or Apple says in the notice that a deployment with a client sends you to that provider and back, and that this prototype has none, rather than pretending a hand-off happened and landing on the workspace. Before the port, confirm the Rauthy deployment has Google and Apple upstreams, WebAuthn and mail switched on. If any of them is off, delete that control rather than ship a dead one.

### Workspace

A 248px sidebar, a 64px topbar, and a two-column area:

- Sidebar, top to bottom: the wordmark and version, the project switcher, the domains, then the person, whose own menu carries `Profile`, `Role`, `Settings` and `Sign out`. The wordmark is at the top left because the product is what a reader looks for first; the project sits under it because it decides what the domains below are about.
- Domains: `Overview`, `Chat`, `Office & Org`, in that order. `Overview` and `Chat` are built screens. `SDLC Pipeline` is gone because its board now lives on the overview, and `Knowledge & Trace` is removed rather than left as a dead row. The rail collapses to a 64px icon strip from a toggle at the left of the topbar, keeps each icon's name in a tooltip, and remembers the choice. It collapses at every width, because the toggle is the reader's, not the layout's; below 900px an expanded rail overlays the page over a scrim instead of squeezing the content into 140px, and a narrow window starts collapsed.
- Topbar: the collapse toggle, a breadcrumb of the project and the view, the same two control buttons as the landing page, then a notices bell. The project name gives way with an ellipsis first, because the view name is how a reader knows where they are when the rail shows only marks. The bell sits beside the language control rather than in the page, because it is chrome and every screen carries it; its dot marks something unread, and opening the list clears it.
- Notices: the bell opens a list drawn from the board rather than invented: what is waiting for a person, what is being worked on, what was approved, and what has not started. Three rows show, one control reveals the rest, and choosing a row opens the stage it names. The sheet is the same popover as the two menus, so Escape, a click outside and Tab out all close it. It also carries its own close mark, because a reader on a touch screen should not have to find the bell again, and it stops at the height of a short screen and scrolls inside itself rather than running off the bottom.
- The page scrolls inside its own column, and that scrollbar is a hairline rather than the platform's grey trough bolted to the edge: a transparent 10px track and a rounded thumb inset to 4px in the page's muted ink at 40%, one weight throughout, because a scrollbar reports position rather than inviting a press. The gutter is always reserved, so a page that grows tall enough to scroll does not shift its text sideways, and the thumb keeps a 44px floor so it can still be found on a long document. It is the one piece of chrome no variable can reach, so the prototype names the WebKit pseudo-elements and falls back to a thin, tinted scrollbar where they are absent.
- Overview: a band at the top holding the readings and the one chart side by side, then the board. Two columns, the stage list on the left and the selected stage on the right. Nothing else sits below the board: the documents a stage produced open under its own row, and the folder list is not on this screen at all. A file tree is a tool for looking something up, not a thing to read at rest, so it opens on demand in a column at the right edge, from a `Files` button in the page head beside the project name, closed until asked for.
- Top band, left: three numbers counted from the board itself. Documents produced and how many stages hold them, stages approved out of nine, and how many stages are waiting on a person. They sit in one row inside their own card, one per column with a hairline between them, each label one word, `Documents`, `Approved`, `Waiting`, and the qualifying phrase under the figure. A label that wraps to three lines pushes its number down and the three figures stop sharing a line, which is the one thing a row of readings must not do. The card takes the smaller share of the band, because three short figures at 26px need about 320px and no more.
- Top band, right: `Project Contribution`, a year of days as a calendar, beside the readings rather than under them, because the two answer the same question at two scales: how far the lifecycle has got, and when the work happened. One column a week, Sunday at the top, one square a day, and five steps of the page's primary blue for how many actions the office recorded that day, summed over everyone who worked. The first step is the track, the next three are the primary mixed into the card at 18, 38 and 62 percent, and the last is the primary itself, so the busiest day is the colour of the product's own button and the quiet ones fade into the page rather than into a second palette. The ramp is derived from `--primary` rather than written as four hex values, so a theme change moves the whole year with it. The window is the last twelve months ending today, so it does not drift while the page is open; the first column starts on the Sunday on or before the first day, and the days before it are void rather than a short column that would misstate the weekday. Month names sit over the columns they start, at least four columns apart so one name cannot run into the next, and the row is dropped when the card is narrower than 500px, which is a question about the card rather than the window. Under the grid, four names with their counts, because the calendar cannot say who made the marks; the foot gives the total and the dates.
- The band is two cards until the shared width stops paying. The readings need 320px to keep three figures on one line; the calendar needs each of its 53 columns to stay wide enough that a day is a square rather than a speck. At 1440 the band gives the calendar about 740px and a cell about 10px, at 1280 about 630px and 8px. Below 1240px the two stack, readings first, because the workspace itself has less width to share there and two narrow cards would each be a squeezed version of itself. Stacked, the calendar gets the full width back and its cells return to about 10px at 900 and 7px at 620. At phone widths a year cannot be drawn at ten pixels a day: at 390 the squares are about 3px, the year reads as texture, and the names and the total are what carry the meaning.
- Why a calendar and not a bar list: the bars answered "how much" and said nothing about "when", which is what a year of work is read for, a quiet stretch and then a run of dark squares before an approval. An earlier version of this calendar was 364 marks wide and scrolled sideways; this one divides its card into 53 columns and shrinks the cell instead, and its size is capped at 720px so a wide screen does not turn a year into 20px tiles. The bar list that replaced it in the previous pass is retired, not because it was wrong at its width but because the year's shape was worth the space. The daily count survives as the source of the total, so the calendar and the number cannot disagree.
- Choosing a name under the calendar is the one place the page stops being a reading. It opens a window titled `Work done`: the nine beats as one lane, filled where that employee worked and left as a track where another owner did, so the answer is a position in the lifecycle rather than a number. The lead turns that position into a record, the stages worked on, the documents those stages produced, and how many of them are approved. Under the lane, one row per stage with its code and name, its baseline, its document count and its day, and its state chip. A stage that produced nothing says so through its state alone, because `0 artifacts` beside `Not started` is the same fact twice. The window is a native `<dialog>` opened from that name; closing it, by Escape or its own button, returns the caret to it.
- Board left: the nine stages with code, name, phase and owner, and a state chip. A stage waiting on a person carries a green marker at the left edge.
- Board right: the selected stage. Header with code and chip, a four-column meta strip (phase, owner, baseline, artifacts), the checklist of what was verified, and the gate.
- Stage rows are a disclosure and a selection at once. A row carries a chevron and opens the documents that stage produced, under itself, indented to the row's text column and tinted to the page colour; the same click selects the stage and shows its detail on the right. Clicking the open row closes it and keeps the selection, so one stage's detail can be read beside a shut list of nine. The stage waiting on a person is open on load, because it is the one a reader came to deal with. Each document under a row is a button, and choosing it opens that file in the right pane, so reading it never shuts the list it came from; the row's own click stops at the row, so a document never doubles as a selection. The list is redrawn on every toggle, so the caret is put back on the same row, or a reader working from the keyboard would land on the page body after each press. Documents used to be a section of their own under the board, repeating the same rows: folding them into the rows removes the second list and the question of which one to read.
- The way into the chat is a `Chat` button at the top right of the overview, in the row that carries the project name. A chat box under the board was removed: it duplicated the chat page in a smaller frame and pushed the board up for no answer a page would not give better. The button is the one thing on the overview that leads somewhere else, so it carries an outline rather than the filled blue: the gate on the right already holds the screen's one filled action, and two blue buttons in one head would make neither of them the next thing to press. It opens the chat page and puts the caret in the composer, because a reader who asked for the chat has already decided to type.
- Chat, the page: a conversation history at the left, the transcript in the middle, and the document an answer cites at the right, above 1280px. The composer sits inside the transcript column rather than under the page, so it ends where the transcript ends and never runs beneath the history. The page head keeps the project name and its one-line description, because the conversation is about that project and a page that dropped it would read as a different screen. The list is titled `Chats` and its one action is a plus at the far right of that title: the word names what the column holds, and the plus starts a conversation in the place a reader looks after reading it. A 28px strip stands against the column's right edge, between the list and the transcript, carrying one mark: the panel outline, which puts the column away and brings it back, level with the column's own head. The mark is small because the list is a column of the room rather than a primary action, and a 36px tile beside a 216px column reads as a second, competing button. It sits outside the column on purpose, because a control inside the thing it hides disappears with it and leaves no way back; there is no second mark in the column's head, and this one mark's label and `aria-expanded` say which way it will go. While the column is away the plus becomes the second mark on the same strip, so starting a conversation never depends on the list being visible. Above 1280px the column is put away and brought back in place; below 1280px the same column is a drawer over the transcript, opened and closed by that button, Escape, the scrim, or choosing a conversation. Below 1120px the cited document does the same, so the transcript keeps the width. The plus is one element that moves between that title and the strip, never two copies, so the two placements cannot drift. A mark that starts a conversation must not vanish with the panel that lists them. Below 1280px, pressing it also puts the drawer away, because the conversation it just started is behind it.
- History: the project opens with three conversations, so the column shows what a history is rather than an empty list: what the project builds, the stage waiting on a person, and the folder an agent may use. Each row carries its title and its message count, and the open one is marked. Selecting a row swaps the transcript and drops a reply still on its way, because that reply belongs to the conversation that asked for it. `New chat` adds a row with no title; the title becomes the first question, shortened with an ellipsis at 44 characters. Each row also carries a removal mark at its end, muted until the row is hovered or the mark has focus, and it asks in a window that names the conversation, with `Keep` focused and `Delete` in red, because a history is the reader's own record and a second press on the same mark is one stray double click away from losing it. Escape or `Keep` leaves the conversation alone, and a confirmed delete returns the caret to the next row. Removing the open conversation opens the one that takes its place; removing the last one leaves a line saying there is none, and the composer still starts a conversation on the first message. Copy uses the translation keys for the three seeded conversations and the typed text for the rest, so a language change reseeds the example and keeps nothing typed, which is the price of a prototype whose example is copy.
- The transcript opens with one exchange per seeded conversation, and a question typed into the composer is answered by a script: the composer shows the working record with its first step running, then the reply lands a moment later, drawn from the same example data the board shows, so the question about the waiting stage names that stage and the question about folders names the path. The script matches a topic from the words and answers it, and a question outside the project says that rather than inventing one. The note under the composer says the replies are written into the prototype rather than generated, because a conversation that looked live would be claiming a model that is not there.
- The composer is one field holding three things: a paperclip at the left, where the reader starts typing, the box, and `Send` at the right. Above it sits one row: the chips of what is attached on the left and the model the question would go to on the right. The model is a pill showing the chosen name, opening upward into a list with a tick on the current one; the names are data, like a project name, so they stay as written in both languages, and the list says under itself that choosing one changes the label only, because there is no model behind the page to switch to. A file joins the sentence rather than becoming a second message, and arrives either from the paperclip or by being dropped anywhere on the composer, which draws a dashed edge and one line telling the reader to let go while a file is over it. A chosen file appears above the field as a chip with its name, its size and a removal mark, and the message that carries it keeps those names under its text, because the sentence alone would not say a file was ever there. Choosing the same file twice is one chip, not two. Nothing is uploaded: the prototype keeps the file in the page and makes no claim about it being sent, and the port attaches the chosen files to the message request and shows the name and size the store returns. At phone width three controls leave the invitation too little room to be read, so `Send` becomes the arrow a chat box wears at that size, its name kept for a screen reader, and the placeholder shortens from `Ask about this project` to `Ask anything` under 560px and to the field's own name under 340px, because a sentence clipped under the caret is worse than a shorter one. The brackets came from measurement: at 390 the box has room for the long sentence in English and not in Indonesian, and at 320 it has room for one word.
- Every scripted answer that came from a document says which one twice. The sentence carries a small numbered mark where the claim is made, and a row of numbered pills under the text names the documents, because a source is only useful beside the words it supports and a reader should not have to match a list to a paragraph by eye. The mark and its pill are one fact seen twice, so pointing at or focusing either lights the other, and either opens the document. The pane shows the sentence the answer came from set apart at the top, the kind of document and its path above it, and under the quote the chunk that sentence was cut from: the place in the file, the lines, which chunk of how many, and when that chunk was indexed. That detail is what makes a quote checkable rather than decorative, since the answer was written from a chunk of a file and the reader can see which chunk and how old the reading is. The number stays lit, in the sentence and in the row, while its document is the one on show. The pane closes with Escape, its close mark, or the files button taking it back for the tree.
- Why one pane rather than two: the files and the cited document never need to be read at the same time, and a second 304px column beside a 216px history and a 248px rail leaves the transcript 436px at 1280. One pane keeps the transcript readable and makes the citation a swap rather than a stack. Two alternatives were rejected: citations as footnotes under the transcript, which puts the passage far from the sentence that cited it, and a modal over the page, which hides the answer that made the claim.
- A cited document belongs to the conversation. Leaving the chat closes the document pane and the history drawer, while the file tree stays open, because a tree is a tool for every screen and a citation is not.
- The working behind an answer is a record rather than a checklist of claims. `How this answer was made` opens to one row per step, each row carrying the commands that step ran and what each command printed, and the summary counts the steps that finished: `3 of 3 done`. A step carries one of four marks. A tick means it finished, a cross means it finished on a command that failed, a dot means it is running, and a hollow ring means it has not started; the same four words sit at the right of every command, so the state is readable without relying on colour alone. When a command fails it keeps the message it printed, because the next command was chosen to get around it: the seeded answer tries `avrig doc read architecture.md`, says `error: no such file`, then finds `design/System-Architecture.md` with one search. That is what reading an unfamiliar project looks like, and showing only the command that worked would be a tidier record than the work. While a reply is on its way the same record is open, with the first step running and the rest not started, and a command that has not run says `not started` rather than carrying a result it does not have. A command never wraps mid-token, so `--json` stays whole, and the row wraps the command above its state word when the column is narrow. A spinning mark is the only animation in the record, and it stops under `prefers-reduced-motion`. Copy sits under each message and appears on hover or keyboard focus, and on a touch screen it stays visible, because an invisible target is still a target; the clipboard write falls back to `execCommand` on a browser that refuses the async one, and the mark confirms itself in place for a moment instead of raising a toast over the conversation.

The project is named by whoever created it, so `My Software Engineering project` is data, not a label: it is never translated, it is set in the text face rather than the mono one, and it wraps in the rail rather than truncating, because a name the reader cannot finish is not a label. In the breadcrumb it is the half that shortens.

The gate is the point of the screen. It has four mutually exclusive states:

| State | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Already approved | The record: who signed, which baseline, when. No button. |
| Held | Which earlier stage must be approved first, by code and name. |
| Approved elsewhere in the sequence | `No gate is open at this stage.` |
| Open | One sentence, then `Verify baseline`, then `Approve baseline {version}`, disabled until verification passes. |

Verification takes a moment and says `Verifying`. It either confirms `Baseline matches v12` and enables approval, or fails with `Baseline does not match`, a note that the artifact changed after the baseline was cut, and approval stays disabled. The failure is reachable at `?state=verify-error`.

Approval opens a native `<dialog>`: the stage, the baseline, the signer, the version, the time, and the statement that an agent cannot sign. Confirming writes the record, moves the stage to approved, updates the count and removes the green marker. Escape closes the dialog and focus returns to the button that opened it; on confirm, focus moves to the gate heading so a screen reader hears the new record.

`Office & Org` is the one unbuilt domain and it is not faked. Selecting it hides the built screens and says this prototype covers software engineering, with a button back to the project. The account menu carries four rows: `Profile`, `Role`, `Settings`, a rule, then `Sign out` in red. The two rows about the person come first, because they are read more often than the window about the office. `Settings` opens a window: a title bar with a close mark, sections on the left, and the chosen section on the right. It is a native `<dialog>` opened with `showModal`, so Escape closes it, the page behind it cannot be reached, and `closedby="any"` closes it on a click outside. It covers the workspace rather than replacing it, so closing returns the reader to the screen they were on, with the caret back on the account button. The sections are ten, in the order a person sets an office up: `Provider`, `Model`, `Agent`, `Skills`, `Tools`, `Workflow`, then, under a hairline, `Office`, `Organization` and `Employee`, then, under a second, `Security`. The first six say what the office runs on; the next three are the office itself, the organization it belongs to, and who works in it, in the order a reader widens out from the office to the people; `Security` comes last because it guards the account rather than describes the work. `Office` and `Organization` drop the word `Setting` because the window is already titled `Settings`. Each is a button carrying `aria-current`, focused on open, and each says what it will hold and, under a hairline, that it is not designed yet: no fake controls behind a window that looks real. `Provider` opens selected, because it is the first thing a new office has to answer. Below 620px the window fills the screen and the sections sit in one row above the panel, because two columns cannot share 390px. The breadcrumb keeps naming the project and the open panel, so no view is ever unlabelled. `Profile` opens a smaller window of the same kind: one face, the name, the role, a display name field and a photo control, and a line saying that email and password belong to the account that signs in. The name and the photo really change in the prototype: the photo is a file the browser holds as an object URL, and both show up at once in the rail avatar, the account button and the window itself. They do not survive a reload, and the window says so rather than implying a store. `Role` is not a second window: it is a row whose choices open as a flyout to the right of the menu, `Founder` and `Architecture Leader`, each with a radio mark on the current one. The flyout keeps the account menu four rows tall and puts the choice where a submenu is expected, outward in the reading direction, instead of growing the menu downward over the rows below it. Below 560px there is no room beside the menu, so the list folds back under its row as a plain group. Choosing one rewrites the rail, the profile window and the signer line of the approval dialog, because the role is data about the signed-in person rather than a label on one screen.

The right pane has three jobs: the files of the project, the document an answer cites, and a document the board lists, opened from the stage that produced it. Above 1120px it takes its own column and the page narrows to make room; below that there is no room to give, so it comes over the page over a scrim and closes with Escape, its own close mark, or a tap outside it. A citation keeps a 304px column, because beside it is an answer the reader is still reading; a file being read takes 384px, because a page of SQL or Markdown needs the width and a quoted sentence does not. The files view carries the tree; the citation view carries the quoted passage and its chunk; the document view carries the file itself, in its own markers, with the stage, the version and the day it was stored above it, and says so plainly when the file is not text instead of showing noise. Its rows are example files and the note under the pane says so, because a page cannot read a folder on the machine; the desktop shell replaces it with a real listing of the project's first folder. A project with no folder says that instead of showing a tree. The citation passages are example content too, and the stage one reads from the same stage the board shows, so the citation and the board cannot disagree.

Sign out is the last row of the account menu, under its own rule and drawn in red, rather than a button beside the operator's name. A button next to the name is one pixel from a harmless click and fires on the first press; a menu is a place a reader arrives on purpose. It still asks before it acts: a dialog titled `Sign out?` says the person goes back to the landing page and nothing in the lifecycle changes, with `Stay` and `Sign out`. The menu closes before the dialog opens, so two panes never sit over one another. `Stay` and Escape both close the dialog and return focus to the account button. Confirming leaves for `/`.

### Projects

A project is a name, a description and the folders it may touch. It is the first control in the rail, under the wordmark, because it decides what everything under it is about.

- The button shows a folder mark, the project name and a chevron, and names itself `Project: My Software Engineering project. Change project.` The name wraps onto as many lines as it needs; it is never truncated.
- The menu lists the projects as one choice among them, the current one checked, then `Project settings` and `New project`. Each row carries the name and its first folder, because the path is what separates two similarly named projects.
- Every row ends in a mark at the far right, and the mark tells the truth about what it does. A project with no lifecycle gets a trash mark: it asks `Delete project?`, names the project, says its name, description and folders leave avrig and that nothing on disk is deleted, and opens with focus on `Keep project`, so a stray Enter keeps the project rather than destroying it. Escape, a backdrop click and `Keep project` all leave the list untouched. `Delete project` is the only red fill in the product.
- A project that holds a lifecycle gets an archive mark instead, because deleting it would throw the lifecycle away. Its dialog is `Archive project?`, explains that the lifecycle stays exactly as it is and that the project can be restored later, and its action is a monochrome `Archive project`, not red, because archiving is reversible. The prototype refuses a delete of a project with a lifecycle even if something calls for one.
- Archived projects move to a labelled `Archived` section at the bottom of the same menu, dimmed, with their name and first folder and one restore mark. Restoring puts the project back in the list and, when no project was open, opens it.
- Deleting or archiving the open project moves the view to the project that takes its place, or, when none is left, to the no-project state: the rail's project button reads `No project`, the breadcrumb reads `No project`, the heading is `No project yet`, and one card explains what a project is and offers `New project`. There is no dead end, and `Project settings` is disabled because there is nothing to edit. The no-project state is the only empty state that carries the action that leaves it.
- `Project settings` opens a dialog with the name, the description, and one path per row with a remove button, plus `Add folder` under the list. Name is the only required field and the browser enforces it: submitting empty keeps the dialog open, marks the field `:user-invalid`, and puts the caret back in it.
- A folder is chosen, not typed. `Add folder` opens a folder picker: a path bar with `Up one level`, the folders inside the current folder, and `Use this folder`. A path row also carries its own browse button, which opens the picker at that path. `Use this folder` writes the path in and puts the caret at its end; Cancel and Escape leave the form untouched. Saving drops a path that appears twice.
- The picker is example data and says so under the list. A web page cannot read a real path off the disk, so the tree is fixed and the choice it returns is a string. The desktop shell replaces it with the native directory dialog, which returns a real path.
- The dialog closes with `Save project`, `Cancel` or Escape, and Escape changes nothing. A click on the backdrop does not close it, because a stray click would throw away typed text. The picker, which holds nothing typed, does close on a backdrop click.
- Projects are data, not copy. The name, the description and the paths are never translated, and the name reaches the rail, the breadcrumb, the `h1`, the page title and the browser title.
- A project with no lifecycle shows the empty state instead of an empty stage list, because a list with nothing in it reads as a failure to load.

The menu is a native popover opened from the button with `command="toggle-popover"`, so the browser anchors it to the project button and flips it rather than letting it leave the screen. Under 560px the account menu, whose button is at the foot of the rail, spans the screen above itself. Its items sit in a `focusgroup`, so the arrow keys, Home and End walk them; entering the menu on open is the only thing script does, and only when the open came from the keyboard. Two behaviours are script because the browser does not supply them: Tab moves into the first item and then out, and the menu closes when focus leaves it and its button, so a walk through the page never leaves a menu hanging open over it.

The goal is the project definition in Codex and ChatGPT, and this is deliberately narrower: a name, a description and folders, with no instructions file, no chat history and no sharing, because none of those exist in this product yet.

## System

Colour roles, defined in `chrome.css` under `:root` and overridden under `[data-theme="dark"]`:

| Role | Light | Dark |
|---|---|---|
| background | `hsl(210 14% 98%)` | `hsl(210 14% 7%)` |
| foreground | `hsl(210 16% 9%)` | `hsl(210 10% 96%)` |
| card | `hsl(0 0% 100%)` | `hsl(210 12% 9%)` |
| muted-foreground | `hsl(210 8% 42%)` | `hsl(210 7% 62%)` |
| border | `hsl(210 12% 89%)` | `hsl(210 9% 19%)` |
| primary | `#0b5cff` | `#6f9dff` |
| primary foreground | `#ffffff` | `#08101f` |
| secondary / accent | `hsl(210 13% 94%)` | `hsl(210 10% 14%)` / `16%` |
| ring | `hsl(168 48% 30%)` | `hsl(168 52% 58%)` |
| gate accent | `#1c7a63` | `#4fbfa1` |
| gate foreground | `#ffffff` | `#08101f` |
| track | `hsl(210 12% 92%)` | `hsl(210 9% 17%)` |

The gate accent is used only where a human approves: the approved chips, the marker on a stage waiting for a person, the shield, the signed record, the success dot in knowledge results, and the filled `Sign approval` and `Approve baseline` buttons. If a new surface needs emphasis that is not an approval, it gets the blue primary, not the green. Errors use the one red pair, `--danger` and `--danger-soft`. `--track` is a quiet day in the contribution calendar and the unfilled beats of the ownership lane, so both read as absence rather than as a colour.

Blue is the ordinary action colour now, not a rare accent: every filled button a screen offers for its main task is blue, on every surface, including the identity provider's `Sign in`. The header's short `Workspace` button is the same blue at 36px. The token pair is `--primary` and `--primary-fg`; `.btn-accent` was retired when the two became the same value, because a class that renders identically to another is a second name for nothing. White on `#0b5cff` measures 6.3:1 and the dark pair measures 7.3:1, so the label is readable in both themes.

A colour that means two things means neither. Green is approval, blue is action, red is destruction. The contribution calendar does not add a fourth hue: its shades are four mixes of `--fg` into `--card`, so a busy day is darker ink on the same page rather than a colour with its own meaning. The green heat ramp of the first pass stays retired for that reason. The focus ring is the one overlap left: `--ring` sits in the same hue family as the gate, so a focused control is ringed in green as well. It is kept because a ring must be visible against a blue button and against the page, and a neutral ring loses that on one of the two; a ring is a mark of where the keyboard is, not a claim about what happened, so the overlap is stated here rather than paid for with a weaker ring.

Type: Archivo, weights 400, 500, 600. Display `clamp(2.4rem, 5vw, 3.9rem)` at line height 1.02 and tracking -0.035em. Section headings `clamp(1.6rem, 2.6vw, 2.15rem)`.

The interface scale is deliberately small and deliberately floored:

| Size | Use |
|---|---|
| 12px | meta labels, chips, codes, counts |
| 12.5px | secondary meta in the workspace |
| 13px | control labels, notes |
| 13.5px | list rows, body in panels |
| 14px | workspace navigation, buttons |
| 14.5px to 15.5px | landing body |
| 17px to 19px | panel headings |
| 22px to 24px | page titles |

Nothing renders below 12px. Below that, at these weights and on a laptop panel, the labels stop being readable and people zoom the page instead, which breaks the layout the sizes were chosen to fit. Monospace is reserved for stage codes, versions, artifact counts and timestamps, never for prose.

Spacing: a 4px scale only. Section rhythm is 64px on small screens and 80px from 1024px up. Content column is 72rem.

Shape: 8px radius on small controls, 12px on cards and list rows, 16px on panels and the dialog. Hairlines rather than shadows, except the board window on the landing page and the dialog, which carry one soft shadow so they read as objects above the page.

Motion: colour transitions only, a 150ms hover, a 700ms spinner. Smooth scrolling is disabled under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, and the spinner slows down rather than stopping, because a frozen spinner reads as a hang.

## Controls

One button per control, and the button changes in place. There is no group, no track, and no thumb.

- Theme: a mark only, 32px square, naming the mode in force through a sun, a monitor or a moon. A click moves to the next mode in the order light, system, dark, and round again.
- Language: a globe and the code in force, `EN` or `ID`. A click swaps the other way. Both codes are two characters, so the button measures 68px in either and the header never shifts.
- The theme button gives up its word because three marks are read faster than one word in a header. The word survives where there is room, in the `aria-label` and the `title`: `Colour theme: System. Change to Dark.`
- Both strings translate: `Tema warna: Sistem. Ganti ke Gelap.` The language button keeps its code as it is, because a code is not a word in any language.
- Plain buttons, so Tab reaches both and Enter or Space activates them. No roving tab index, no arrow keys, nothing to learn.
- Both are always visible, never behind a menu, on every surface including 360px wide.

The cost of one button: reaching dark from light passes through system. That is visible rather than hidden, because the button names the next mode before you press it. On the identity provider screen these two controls are the design and not the implementation: that page allows no injected script, so the port restyles Rauthy's own controls to this shape instead of replacing them.

## Language policy

Chrome and action labels translate: navigation, buttons, state chips, notices, gate sentences, the approval dialog.

Product domain and stage names stay English in both languages: `Chat`, `Office & Org`, `Enterprise Data Model`, `Requirements Engineering`. They are the product's own nouns, they appear in the plan files and the issue tracker, and translating them would give one thing two names.

This conflicts with the line in PRODUCT.md that console strings are inline English by design. It is flagged rather than reconciled: the prototype shows the split, and PRODUCT.md needs the same sentence updated before the port.

The product says `software engineering` for the activity and `lifecycle` for one run through the nine stages. `Delivery` is retired, and so is `pengiriman`, which in Indonesian means shipping something. Prose calls the lifecycle systematic rather than ordered: the stages cannot be skipped, which is what systematic states. Indonesian prose translates both words while `SDLC` stays an identifier of the plan and the issue tracker.

## Honesty rules applied

- Every number is a version, a count of stages, or a timestamp inside a record.
- The workspace screens carried an `Example data` chip in the corner, and the landing still carried the same words in its window bar. Both are removed at the user's request: a label on every screen reads as chrome and stops being read. What replaces it is disclosure where the claim is made. The chat says under its composer that the replies are written into the prototype, the files view says its tree is an example, the document view says its passage is an example, and the folder picker says so under its list. The landing keeps the two `Example record` captions under its rows, because those explain what a record shows rather than labelling a whole screen. The overview closes each of its two cards with its own note: the readings are counted from the stages on the page, and the contributions are one per document or check an agent produced. The port must not quietly drop the remaining notes, because the board's numbers, the stage records, the contribution counts, the file tree and the four cited documents are fixed example content rather than readings from a store.
- No customer, testimonial, benchmark, latency, uptime or agent count appears, because none of those exist yet.
- The workspace's connection row says `connected` because the prototype is a still; it sits at the bottom right of the content column, and the port must bind it to the real socket state or hide it.
